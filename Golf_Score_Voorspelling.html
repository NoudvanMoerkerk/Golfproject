4. Missing data analysis (MCAR / MAR / MNAR)

Before i just drop rows with missing values, i want to figure out why* the sg_* columns are missing. I will use the link from the workshop of Hans of exploration 5: missing data types:

  • MCAR, Missing Completely At Random: the fact that a value is missing has nothing to do with the observation being studied (e.g. a row lost to a transfer error).
  • MAR, Missing At Random: the missing variable is related to some other observed variable in the data, but not to the missing value itself (e.g. income is reported less often by a certain age group).
  • MNAR, Missing Not At Random: the missing variable is related to the missing value itself (e.g. people with high debt are less likely to report their debt).

There are diffrent ways to handle the missing values, here are the ways we could choose(We will choose one of these later): ignore/delete the item, use the feature's mean from all available data, fill in a special value (e.g. -1), use the mean from similar items, or use another model to predict the missing value (imputation) — plus more formal approaches like Maximum Likelihood Estimation, or partial deletion (listwise/pairwise).

Let's check whether our missing sg_ values look random, or follow a pattern we can explain with other columns.

The conclusion is that this is MAR (Missing At Random), not MCAR or MNAR.

The missing data is not random but it follows a clear pattern. Some tournaments are missing this data for 100% of players (usually smaller or international events, like the Puerto Rico Open), while other tournaments have 0% missing (usually the big, well-known events). The amount of missing data also drops over time, from 35% in 2015 to around 15% in recent years, and tournaments without a cut miss more data (30%) than regular tournaments (20%).

The explanation for this is probably that some tournaments simply weren't covered by the PGA Tour's tracking system (smaller events, older years, fewer cameras). This means the missing data is connected to things we can see in the data, like which tournament or season it is not to the players performance.

  • It's not MNAR, because there's no reason a player's own performance would affect whether the tournament was tracked.
  • It's not MCAR either, because the pattern is clearly tied to tournament and season, not just random chance.

I will remove the rows with missing data instead of filling them in with values. The reason for this is since entire tournaments are missing, there is no similar player or round nearby togive a realistic number. Making up values for tournaments i have zero real data on could make my model less accurate, so removing those rows is the more honest choice.

Limitation: because of this, my model only learns from tournaments that were tracked. Mostly bigger, more recent, US-based tournaments. Because we have enough data left over i think the model will still be accurate(Maybe less on smaller tournaments).

9. Evaluating the model

In [14]:
y_pred_train = model.predict(X_train)
y_pred_test = model.predict(X_test)

def evaluate(y_true, y_pred, label):
    r2 = r2_score(y_true, y_pred)
    mae = mean_absolute_error(y_true, y_pred)
    rmse = np.sqrt(mean_squared_error(y_true, y_pred))
    print(f'{label:10s} | R2: {r2:.3f}  MAE: {mae:.3f}  RMSE: {rmse:.3f}')

evaluate(y_train, y_pred_train, 'Train')
evaluate(y_test, y_pred_test, 'Test')

Train      | R2: 0.594  MAE: 1.221  RMSE: 1.531
Test       | R2: 0.593  MAE: 1.219  RMSE: 1.540

This shows how accurate my model is.

  • model.predict(X_train) and model.predict(X_test) let the trained model generate predictions for both the training data and the test data.
  • The evaluate() function Gives me R², MAE, RMSE and prints them out.

What do the 3 numbers mean?

  • R2(0.59)= a number between 0 and 1 that says: what percentage of the variation in scores can the model explain using our four features? 0.59 means about 59% of the variation in scores is explained by how someone played off the tee, on approach, around the green, and putting. The remaining ~41% comes from things we don't measure (weather, course difficulty that day, luck, etc.).
  • MAE (1.22)= Mean Absolute Error: on average, the model's prediction is off by 1.22 strokes from the real score (regardless of whether it was too high or too low).
  • RMSE (1.53-1.54)= similar to MAE, but penalizes large errors more heavily (by squaring the errors before taking the square root). RMSE is always ≥ MAE; the fact that they're close together (1.22 vs 1.53) means there are no extreme outlier errors throwing the model way off.

The most important thing to look at: train vs. test.

Notice how close the train and test results are to each other (R² 0.594 vs 0.593, MAE 1.221 vs 1.219). That's a great sign it means the model is not overfitting. It performs about equally well on data it already knew as on completely new player-tournaments. An overfit model would score much better on the training data than on the test data, because it would have memorized the training data instead of learning the actual pattern.

One more nice detail is that this is also better than the first time i ran this without removing the RBC Canadian Open rows in chapter 5 (R² was 0.53 back then) so removing the RBC Canadian Open rows genuinely improved the model, not just the correlations.

10. Interpretation/ Conclusion

  • Look at the coefficients from chapter 8: a negative coefficient means that playing better on that part of the game (higher strokes-gained) leads to a lower (better) score. In our model, sg_ott (off the tee) has the strongest coefficient (-0.958), meaning tee shots weigh the heaviest in predicting the final score. sg_putt (putting) has the weakest coefficient (-0.919) still clearly important, but slightly less important than the other three. That said, all four coefficients are close together (-0.92 to -0.96), so no single part of the game dominates by a huge margin they all matter roughly as much, but if you want to focus only on one thing i would advise on perfecting yourshot of the tee because that is the most likely to give a better final score. Save the training for your puts for the last because that weighs slightly less heavy on your final score.

  • My R2 is 0.59 this means the model explains about 59% of the variation in scores, with the remaining 41% coming from things we didn't measure (weather, course difficulty, luck, etc.). This is a solid, realistic result for golf performance data, not an unusually high one. Earlier when i talked to Hans we expected the R2 might end up very close to 1 since strokes gained(All the SG Categories summed up) already shows how many strokes you gain. That turned out not to be true which makes for a more interesting model. Strokes gained is measured relative to the field average that round, while our target is the player's absolute score and that absolute score still carries variation that strokes gained can't explain. So this model is a genuinely useful, realistic, not a too easy to predict model with a R2 0f almost 1.

11.1 Is linear regression the right choice?

Before trusting a linear regression model, it's worth checking the assumptions it relies on: that the relationship between each feature and the target is roughly linear, and that the errors (residuals) are randomly scattered around zero with no clear pattern. Let's visualize both.

Each plot shows one feature against the target. All four show the same shape: a loose, downward-sloping cloud of points — higher strokes-gained goes with a lower (better) score, and the trend looks fairly straight rather than curved. There's a lot of spread around that trend (which matches our R2 of 0.59), but no obvious curve or bend that would suggest a non-linear relationship. That's a good sign for using linear regression.

Two things to check in a residual plot: do the residuals stay centered around 0 across the whole range (no funnel shape, no curve), and do they look roughly normally distributed? Both hold up reasonably well here: the residuals stay centered around 0 for the whole range of predicted scores, with no strong funnel or curve shape, and the distribution looks close to a symmetric bell curve (skew close to 0).

Conclusion: based on these diagnostics, linear regression looks like a reasonable choice for this data — the relationships are roughly linear and the residuals behave as expected. The main limitation isn't that linear regression is the wrong type of model, but that it only has access to features that explain part of the story (see 11.2).

Even after tuning, the Random Forest's test R² is 0.587 — still a little below linear regression's 0.593, and its MAE/RMSE are both slightly worse too (1.225 vs 1.219, 1.55 vs 1.54). So the honest answer is: no, a Random Forest doesn't beat my baseline here, even with reasonable tuning.

This isn't really a surprise — it directly confirms what I found back in chapter 11.1: the relationship between each strokes-gained feature and score is already close to a straight line, with no curve or bend. A Random Forest's main advantage is capturing non-linear patterns, but if the real relationship is already linear, there's nothing extra for it to gain, and its added flexibility just risks fitting noise instead of signal.

One interesting side-result: the Random Forest's feature importances (sg_app 0.37, sg_putt 0.30, sg_ott 0.18, sg_arg 0.15) rank the features quite differently from how linear regression's coefficients look (all close to -0.9 to -0.96). That's because they're measuring different things — a coefficient is how much the prediction shifts per unit of strokes gained, while importance reflects how much a feature actually helps tell players apart in practice. Approach play varies the most between PGA Tour pros, so it's the most useful feature for separating good rounds from bad ones, even though, unit-for-unit, all four strokes-gained stats shift the predicted score by about the same amount. That matches common golf analytics wisdom: approach shots are usually considered the biggest separator between tour pros.

Conclusion for 12.2: switching to a more complex model type doesn't help on its own. The real gain in this chapter came from adding a missing feature (course difficulty), not from a fancier model — a useful lesson that more model complexity isn't a substitute for having the right information in the data.
